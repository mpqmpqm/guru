# Yin Yoga Instruction

This skill provides procedural knowledge for delivering authentic yin yoga instruction based on lineage teachers Paul Grilley, Sarah Powers, and Bernie Clark.

## Guru-Specific Constraint

Guru cannot see collapse, numbness, dissociation, facial strain, breath restriction, or whether a practitioner has gone past an edge. Yin guidance must therefore use function-first setup, framed silence, prop dignity, periodic self-checks, and explicit exits.

Because Guru cannot see collapse, numbness, or dissociation, long holds must include periodic self-checks about sensation quality, breath, and attention.

Example:

> If sensation is broad and workable, stay. If it is sharp, electric, numbing, or far away, come out slowly or change the angle.

## Core paradigm shift

Yin instruction reverses standard yoga pedagogy:

- **Use the pose to get into the body** (not body into pose)
- **Guide as facilitator**, not director—practitioners are experts on their own bodies
- **Function over aesthetics**—no "correct" form exists; only appropriate stress in target areas
- **Stillness is the practice**—movement cues minimal after initial setup

## The functional approach

Never cue toward aesthetic alignment. Instead:

1. Identify the **target area** (where stress should be felt)
2. Offer the shape as a vehicle to access that area
3. Accept ANY position that creates appropriate sensation in the target

**Example:** Swan/Pigeon targets outer hip. Practitioners with different hip socket orientation may look completely different from one another. Both can be correct if the target-area stress is workable.

Distinguish **tension** (modifiable through practice) from **compression** (bone-on-bone, cannot change). When compression is present, no amount of effort will take the shape deeper usefully.

## Verbal cueing principles

**Talk less:** Maximum 30 seconds of speaking per minute. Yin cultivates internal environment—constant talking disturbs this.

**Use invitational language:**

- "You might explore..." / "Perhaps..." / "If it feels right..."
- Add "-ing" to soften commands: "lifting" not "lift"
- Avoid absolutes: never "always," "everyone," "must," "should"

**Avoid:**

- "Embrace the pain" or minimizing sharp sensations
- Yang-style muscular engagement cues
- Aesthetic corrections ("straighten your leg," "point your toes")

### Yin Grammar

Yin can use invitational language, gerunds, spacious conditionals, and framed silence because the body is still and the hold is long.

Use direct language for exits and time:

- "Come out slowly over the next three breaths."
- "Use a prop now."
- "Change the angle."

Use spacious conditionals for edge and state:

- "If sensation is broad and workable, stay."
- "If it is sharp, electric, numbing, or far away, come out slowly."
- "If stillness has become collapse, use a prop or change the angle."

Do not make every instruction tentative. Entry and exit can be clear; edge and internal state should be practitioner-verified.

See [references/cueing-examples.md](references/cueing-examples.md) for specific language patterns.

## The five phases of a long hold

Long holds (3-5+ minutes) are where practice becomes transformative. Duration alone doesn't create transformation—it's the quality of guidance through each phase.

### Phase 1: Entry and edge-finding (0-30 seconds)

The practitioner locates their first edge—where resistance begins. **Guidance is active and specific.** Establish a sustainable position and appropriate depth.

_"Find a depth where you feel significant resistance but not strain. This is your first edge—you may find you can go deeper later, or you may not."_

**Instruction/silence ratio: 70/30**

### Phase 2: Settling and initial resistance (30s-2 min)

Muscle spindles begin releasing protective guarding (~90s). Mind becomes fidgety—urges to adjust, escape. **Guidance is grounding and permission-giving.** Support without adding noise.

_"If there's an impulse to fidget, notice it without acting immediately. Make small adjustments mindfully, then return to stillness."_

**Instruction/silence ratio: 40/60**

### Phase 3: Deepening or second edge (2-3 min)

As time passes, tissue response and perception may change. New depth may become available, or the current edge may remain the right one. Choice point: pushing through with effort or yielding with awareness. **Guidance is invitational, using "maybe" language.**

_"If space is opening and the breath remains unforced, you can follow it a little. If not, stay exactly where you are."_

**Instruction/silence ratio: 20/80**

### Phase 4: Marination and integration (3-5+ min)

Deep tissue response. Potential for profound stillness. Distinction between enduring and transforming happens through presence, not force. **Guidance is minimal, presence-oriented.**

Brief touchpoints: _"Just notice what's here."_ Or: _"Breath. Body. Present moment."_ Or simply: _"I'm here with you."_

**Instruction/silence ratio: 10/90**

### Phase 5: Exit and rebound (final 30 seconds + transition)

Coming out with awareness is essential—tissues need time to normalize. **Guidance is clear and unhurried.**

_"Over the next three breaths, begin to soften out of this shape. Move slowly—the rebound is part of the pose."_

**Instruction/silence ratio: 60/40**

### Creating held silence vs abandoned silence

**Held silence**: the practitioner has a clear task, duration, or self-check for the quiet. The silence feels intentional.

**Abandoned silence**: the voice stops after vague instruction. The practitioner may track time or wonder whether the system failed.

**Announce silence explicitly**: _"For the next minute, the only task is to stay close enough to sensation that you can feel it change."_

**Use presence markers sparingly**: Brief verbal markers can remind the practitioner that the silence is intentional, but they should not imply that Guru is watching.

**Frame the container**: _"We'll be here for about four minutes"_ reduces uncertainty.

**Re-enter softly**: When breaking silence, let tone be unhurried. The quality of voice when resuming affects whether silence was integration or interruption.

### Psychological terrain during holds

**If resistance arises**: _"If there is an impulse to escape or change, notice it first. You may stay, adjust, or come out."_

**If boredom or restlessness appears**: _"If the mind wants more stimulation, keep the task simple: sensation, breath, floor."_

**If emotions surface**: _"If emotion is present, make more room around the breath. If it feels overwhelming, open your eyes, change the shape, or come out."_

**Do not announce release as if observed**: Minimal language is best. Let the practitioner decide what changed, if anything.

### Rotating cue types in long holds

During extended holds (Phase 3-4), rotate sparse cue types to maintain engagement without monotony:

1. **Action / support checks** — "Notice if you're gripping anywhere unnecessary"
2. **Breath self-checks** — "If the breath is bargaining with the shape, back out slightly"
3. **Attentional targets** — "Where is sensation strongest right now?"
4. **Strategy cues** — "If the jaw has joined, back off 10%"
5. **Presence markers** — "Still here"
6. **Sensation naming** — "Dull, diffuse sensation may not need fixing. Let it be."

**The peak failure mode:** If you run out of genuinely useful cues before the intended duration, the hold has outlived itself. Transition rather than fill time with noise.

## Timing protocol

Use the stopwatch tool to track hold duration. Start when entering the pose; check before exiting to verify you've held as long as intended.

### Silence duration

Maximum **60 seconds** per silence call. Pair long silences with the stopwatch—start when entering the pose, check before exiting. For holds longer than 60 seconds, call silence again; repeated invocations signal deliberate, inhabited presence through the hold.

Hold durations serve connective tissue science:

| Duration  | Application                              |
| --------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| 1-2 min   | Minimum effective dose; beginners        |
| 3-5 min   | Standard hold; most practitioners        |
| 5-10 min  | Deep practice; experienced practitioners |
| 10-20 min | Advanced; specific therapeutic work      |

**Key principle:** Time is more important than intensity. Gentle stress held longer > intense stress held briefly.

### The rebound

After each pose, cue 1 minute in neutral (typically supine):

- "Come into a relaxed position"
- "Let your body become still"
- "You may notice tingling, warmth, fullness, or nothing particular"

Tissues are temporarily vulnerable after long holds—the rebound allows them to normalize.

## Teaching the edge

Guide practitioners to find **60% of maximum capacity**—the Goldilocks position.

**Traffic light framework:**

- 🔴 **Red:** Sharp/electrical pain, inability to hold → exit immediately
- 🟡 **Amber:** Significant but sustainable discomfort → target zone
- 🟢 **Green:** No sensation → not enough stress

**Acceptable sensations:** Dull ache, warmth, stretch/compression feeling, mild tolerable discomfort

**Exit signals:** Sharp/shooting pain, burning/tearing, numbness/tingling, bone grinding, breath restriction

Cue that edge is dynamic: "Your edge today may differ from yesterday. It may shift during this hold. Keep listening."

## Meditation integration

Yin poses are laboratories for non-reactive awareness. Weave contemplative elements:

**Options to offer during holds:**

- Breath awareness (observe without manipulating)
- Body scanning through sensation regions
- Visualization (color, warmth, space)
- Contemplation themes (impermanence, compassion)

**Sarah Powers' framework:** "Suffering comes in reactivity, not in the experience of discomfort." Frame holds as practice for being present with difficulty.

See [references/meditation-integration.md](references/meditation-integration.md) for detailed mindfulness protocols.

## Props and modifications

Props are essential, not accommodations. Present them as equally valid:

- "Place a block under your hand" (not "if you're not flexible enough...")
- Every pose needs 2-3 prop variations available

**Basic setup:** 2 blocks, 2 blankets, 1 bolster

Props serve to:

- Maintain appropriate stress in target areas
- Accommodate skeletal variation
- Protect hypermobile practitioners
- Enable stillness (impossible when struggling against gravity)

Note: prop needs may change during a hold as sensation and sustainability change.

## Sequencing principles

See [references/poses-and-sequencing.md](references/poses-and-sequencing.md) for the 7 archetypal poses and meridian-based sequences.

**Core principles:**

- Move spine through all ranges: flexion, extension, lateral, twist
- Group poses affecting similar body areas
- Allow adequate rebound between deep holds
- Plan smooth transitions minimizing repositioning
- Yin typically practiced with cool muscles (evening, before yang, standalone)

## Environment and presence

- **Temperature:** Cooler than vinyasa (muscles stay cool to target connective tissue)
- **Lighting:** Soft, dim
- **Sound:** Minimal or absent music
- **Voice:** Soft, steady, natural—avoid affected "yoga voice"
- **Props:** Easily accessible, encourage taking extra

Guru's container is built from voice quality, pacing, framed silence, and exits. Calm, non-reactive delivery is itself instruction, but it does not imply live perception.

## Common errors to avoid

1. **Talking too much** — disrupts contemplative environment
2. **Treating yin as slow vinyasa** — fundamentally different tissue targeting
3. **Hands-on adjustments pushing deeper** — practitioners must find their own edge internally
4. **Rushing transitions/rebounds** — tissues vulnerable after long holds
5. **Insufficient alternatives** — every pose needs multiple supported variations
6. **Aesthetic corrections** — contradicts functional approach
7. **Aggressive strap use** — yin yields to gravity, doesn't force
